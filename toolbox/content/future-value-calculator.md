## How it works

Future value is what one amount becomes after it grows at a constant rate for a number of years.
There are no deposits and no withdrawals. One amount goes in and one amount comes out.

`Projected value = starting value * (1 + rate)^years`

**rate** is the annual growth rate divided by 100. The power is the duration in years. Growth
compounds once a year, so the second year grows the first year's result rather than the original
amount.

1. Enter the starting value.
2. Enter the annual growth rate.
3. Enter the duration in years.
4. Read the projected value and the total growth.

Total growth is the projected value minus the starting value. The chart below the result plots the
value at the end of each year, so you can see where the curve turns steep.

The starting value and the duration must be greater than zero. The growth rate cannot be negative,
and a rate of zero returns the starting value unchanged. The duration may be a fraction of a year.

Amounts follow the currency and the number format you choose in settings. The calculation runs in
your browser.

## Worked examples

### 100,000 growing at 12 percent for 5 years

The projected value is **176,234.17** and the total growth is 76,234.17.

The first year adds 12,000.00, taking the value to 112,000.00. Add a sixth year and the value
reaches 197,382.27. That year adds far more than the first, although the rate never changes. What
changes is the base it works on.

### 2,000,000 growing at 6 percent for 25 years

The projected value is **8,583,741.44** and the total growth is 6,583,741.44.

A rate of 6 percent sounds small next to 12 percent, but 25 years of it more than quadruples the
amount. Duration and rate both sit in the exponent, and neither one dominates on its own.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the difference between this and the compound interest calculator?

This page grows one amount and compounds once a year. The
[Compound Interest Calculator](/compound-interest-calculator) also takes a recurring contribution
and a compounding frequency. Set that page to yearly compounding with a contribution of 0 and the
two agree exactly: 100,000 at 12 percent for 10 years gives 310,584.82 on both.

### Can the duration be a fraction of a year?

Yes. 100,000 at 12 percent for 7.5 years gives 233,956.53. The chart draws a point at the end of
each whole year and then one last point at 7.5.

### Does doubling the growth rate double the result?

No, it does more than that. 100,000 at 12 percent for 5 years reaches 176,234.17, with 76,234.17 of
growth. At 24 percent the same amount reaches 293,162.51, with 193,162.51 of growth. The rate sits
inside a power, so doubling it multiplies the growth by well over two.

### Can I enter a negative growth rate?

No. The lowest rate the page accepts is zero, which returns the starting value unchanged. To
measure a fall, put the two amounts into the [CAGR Calculator](/cagr-calculator) instead. It
reports 250,000 falling to 180,000 over 3 years as −10.37 percent a year.

### Does the result allow for inflation, fees or taxes?

No. The page applies the one rate you enter, for the number of years you enter, and nothing else.
Any cost that reduces the growth has to be inside the rate before you type it.

### Is anything I type stored?

No. There is no account, nothing you type is sent to a server, and no result is written down.
The page works with no internet connection.

## Good to know

Long durations produce numbers that look implausible until you check them. 1,000,000 at 8 percent
for 30 years reaches 10,062,656.89. Nothing unusual happens along the way. The curve is the same
shape as the five-year one, seen further along.

This page and the [CAGR Calculator](/cagr-calculator) are the same equation solved for different
unknowns. This one has the rate and wants the ending value. That one has both values and wants the
rate. For growth fed by a monthly contribution, use the [SIP Calculator](/sip-calculator).
